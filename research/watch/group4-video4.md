## 2. Analyst notes (my own analysis)

**Note on attribution:** the "idiot" reply is posted by the creator's own handle (@filomation). The video title ("Someone's never heard of compound interest") is **deliberately ambiguous about who the "someone" is**: the winner (for not taking the lump sum to compound) or the replier. That ambiguity is the engine. Both camps think the title supports them, so both comment.

**The envelope math the video never shows (my calculations):**
- $1,000/week × 52 = **$52,000/yr**.
- **No-interest breakeven:** $1,000,000 ÷ $52,000 ≈ **19.2 years**. If she collects for more than about 19 years, the annuity pays more in raw dollars.
- **Implied interest rate:** $52,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = **5.2%**. A perpetual $52k/yr is worth exactly $1M at a 5.2% discount rate. That gives the one-line envelope rule: **if you can't reliably earn more than about 5.2%/yr on the $1M after tax, forever, take the $1,000/week.**
- Present value of $52k/yr (my computation; ordinary annuity, annual payments):

| Discount rate | 30 yrs | 40 yrs | 50 yrs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | $1.02M | $1.20M | $1.34M |
| 4% | $0.90M | $1.03M | $1.12M |
| 5% | $0.80M | $0.89M | $0.95M |
| 7% | $0.65M | $0.69M | $0.72M |

  The "right" answer depends on two assumptions you can write on an envelope: **your return rate and how long you'll collect**.
- **Tax wrinkle (general knowledge, not verified in this step):** Canada generally doesn't tax lottery prizes, but investment income earned on a lump sum is taxable. That tilts the comparison toward the annuity relative to a US-style analysis. Inflation tilts it back toward the lump sum, because a fixed $1,000/week loses purchasing power. A video can't settle all of this in 5 s, which is why the comments explode.
- vidIQ's "$2.6M+ over 50 years" is only $52k × 50 in undiscounted dollars. It depends on an age (18) that the video does not show.

**Formula anatomy:**
1. **About 5 s with no narration.** The viewer *must* rewatch to read the headline, cheque and reply, which pushes retention past 100%.
2. **A real-world money dilemma with two defensible answers** (like the "answer is not 300" ambiguity in group 1 video 1, but with real stakes).
3. **A smug wrong-or-right take placed on screen**, so viewers feel compelled to correct someone.
4. **A reaction face** humanises it and sets the emotional tone.
5. **Zero production cost:** screenshot, selfie and trending audio.

**Risks if we copy it:**
- Using a real person's news photo or name (even blurred) carries privacy/likeness risk. We should restage the dilemma generically ("$1M now or $1,000/week for life?") rather than screenshot a real winner.
- Pure rage-bait without resolution builds no trust. A "rough but right" brand needs a payoff, even if it's in a second video.

**How Back of the Envelope can beat it (ideas for the teaser step, not final scripts):**
1. **A 5–7 s silent dilemma card written on an envelope:** "$1M today or $1,000/week for life?". It keeps the replay mechanics, plus an explicit question banner (vidIQ's fix #2).
2. **An "Envelope Answer" follow-up short (10–20 s):** flip the envelope to reveal *two* numbers: **19.2 years** (breakeven) and **5.2%** (implied rate). Close with: "Beat 5.2% forever? Take the million. Can't? Take the cheque." Pin a comment linking the two.
3. **A series of lump-sum-vs-stream dilemmas:** pension lump sum vs monthly, rent vs buy, salary vs signing bonus, "$1 doubled daily for 30 days vs $1M". Each is solved with one envelope line.
4. **A faceless brand reaction.** Replace the creator's face with a recurring envelope "character" or a pen that circles the wrong number. That keeps the reaction beat and stays on brand.
5. **Name the implied rate in the hook text** ("This is a 5.2% bond in disguise"). It reframes the meme as a finance insight, which is the angle no one else in the comments leads with.
