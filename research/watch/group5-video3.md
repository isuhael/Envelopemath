## My analysis: Envelope Math takeaways
- **Format archetype:** "Would you rather: money edition." A two-option dilemma where intuition and math disagree. It is ideal for a math-first channel because the number *is* the plot twist.
- **The riddle is worn out. The structure isn't.** The doubling penny is so well known that many commenters already know the answer. We should keep the dilemma structure and write **fresh dilemmas whose answer most people don't know**, then settle them with on-screen envelope math. Possible prompts (to be worked out and checked in a later step): $1M today vs. $X a day for life; a lump-sum lottery payout vs. the annuity; skipping one daily coffee vs. buying the stock.
- **Steal:** (a) the question stays on screen for the whole video; (b) a 0.5–1 s pause that invites the viewer to pick before we answer ("pause and pick"); (c) the day-15 "still looks like a terrible deal" beat ($163.84) as the tension trough before the curve explodes.
- **Improve:** **reveal the number**, then end on a second, related question so the comment debate keeps going *and* the viewer still gets a payoff. Example (my math, day 1 = $0.01, counting only that day's amount): "...but what if it only doubled for **27** days?" Day 27 = 0.01 x 2^26 = **$671,088.64**, so the $1M wins. Day 28 = $1,342,177.28 is where the penny takes the lead. Cutting just three days flips the answer, which is a strong envelope-math twist. Caveat to state on screen: if you count the cumulative total, the crossover moves one day earlier (26-day total $671,088.63; 27-day total $1,342,177.27). Spelling out "that day's amount" vs. "running total" is itself good comment bait.
- **Envelope twist:** handwrite the doubling on an envelope. It runs out of room around day 10–12, so the doubling spills onto the back of the envelope, then onto a stack of envelopes. The overflow of paper becomes the visual for the exponential.
